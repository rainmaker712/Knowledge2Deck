[Reverse Thinking Makes LLMs Stronger Reasoners](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.19865)

Authors: Justin Chih-Yao Chen, Zifeng Wang, Hamid Palangi, Rujun Han, Sayna Ebrahimi, Long Le, Vincent Perot, Swaroop Mishra, Mohit Bansal, Chen-Yu Lee, Tomas Pfister

> Reverse thinking plays a crucial role in human reasoning. Humans can reason not only from a problem to a solution but also in reverse, i.e., start from the solution and reason towards the problem. This often enhances overall reasoning performance as it enables consistency checks between their forward and backward thinking. To enable Large Language Models (LLMs) to perform reverse thinking, we introduce Reverse-Enhanced Thinking (RevThink), a framework composed of data augmentation and learning objectives. In RevThink, we augment the dataset by collecting structured forward-backward reasoning from a teacher model, consisting of: (1) the original question, (2) forward reasoning, (3) backward question, and (4) backward reasoning. We then employ three objectives to train a smaller student model in a multi-task learning fashion: (a) generate forward reasoning from a question, (b) generate a backward question from a question, and (c) generate backward reasoning from the backward question. Experiments across 12 datasets covering commonsense, math, and logical reasoning show an average 13.53% improvement over the student model's zero-shot performance and a 6.84% improvement over the strongest knowledge distillation baselines. Moreover, our method demonstrates sample efficiency -- using only 10% of the correct forward reasoning from the training data, it outperforms a standard fine-tuning method trained on 10x more forward reasoning. RevThink also exhibits strong generalization to out-of-distribution held-out datasets.

## Follow-Up Questions

1. Why does reverse thinking improve reasoning performance in humans, and what does this suggest about the limitations of forward-only chain-of-thought prompting in LLMs?

2. RevThink trains the student model on three objectives simultaneously: generating forward reasoning, generating a backward question, and generating backward reasoning. How might conflicting gradients across these objectives affect training stability, and what does multi-task learning contribute beyond simply fine-tuning on combined data?

3. The method achieves strong performance using only 10% of correct forward reasoning examples, outperforming a standard fine-tuning model trained on 10x more data. What mechanisms could explain this sample efficiency — is it the backward signal itself, or the structure of the training objectives?

4. How is the "backward question" constructed from an original question and its answer, and what makes a good backward question for testing consistency between forward and backward reasoning chains?

5. RevThink relies on a teacher model to generate structured forward-backward reasoning pairs. What are the risks of this approach if the teacher model makes systematic errors in its backward reasoning, and how might such errors propagate to the student?

6. The paper evaluates across commonsense, math, and logical reasoning datasets. Are there reasoning domains where backward thinking would be less applicable or potentially harmful — for instance, where the reverse problem is ill-defined?

7. Given the results showing improvements on out-of-distribution datasets, what does RevThink’s generalization suggest about whether backward reasoning teaches domain-specific skills or a more general reasoning capability?

## Answers

**1.** Reverse thinking in humans functions as a consistency verification mechanism: by reasoning from the known solution back to the original problem, one can detect logical gaps or errors in the forward chain that might otherwise go unnoticed. For LLMs, forward-only chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting can generate plausible-sounding but internally inconsistent reasoning steps, because the model is never forced to verify that its solution is uniquely supported by its stated logic. Without a backward pass, there is no signal pushing the model to produce only forward reasoning chains that are logically reversible. RevThink exploits this by explicitly training models to generate backward questions (what problem would this solution come from?) and backward reasoning, effectively teaching bidirectional consistency as a learned skill.

**2.** In multi-task learning, different objectives can produce conflicting gradient signals when the loss surfaces for each task point in different directions in parameter space. RevThink mitigates this by framing the three objectives as structurally related tasks — all operate over the same reasoning chain — which encourages shared representations rather than purely competitive gradient updates. Beyond simple combined fine-tuning, multi-task learning’s contribution here is the inductive bias it provides: the model is explicitly trained to understand the relationship between question, forward reasoning, backward question, and backward reasoning as a unified structure, rather than treating each as independent input-output pairs. This relational structure is what enables the model to generalize the consistency check rather than merely memorizing forward reasoning patterns.

**3.** The sample efficiency likely arises primarily from the backward signal itself rather than the structure of objectives alone. When the model is trained to generate backward questions and backward reasoning, it must implicitly understand the logical structure of the problem deeply enough to invert it — this is a harder task than simply learning to produce forward chains, and solving it forces richer internal representations. With only 10% of correct forward examples, the backward objective compensates by providing more informative learning signal per example: the model must learn not just "what reasoning leads to this answer" but also "what problem class does this reasoning structure belong to." This self-consistency pressure is analogous to contrastive or self-supervised learning, where harder objectives yield stronger representations more efficiently.

**4.** The backward question is constructed by essentially asking: "Given that the answer to a question is X, what is the original question (or a variant of it) that would be answered by this reasoning chain?" A good backward question preserves the structural difficulty of the original problem while being uniquely solvable using the forward reasoning in reverse. For math problems, a strong backward question might reconstruct missing premises from a derived result; for commonsense reasoning, it might identify which causal scenario would produce the observed outcome. The paper uses a teacher model to generate these, implying that backward question quality depends heavily on the teacher’s ability to identify the logical inverse of each problem — which is itself a non-trivial generation task.

**5.** If the teacher model makes systematic errors in backward reasoning — for instance, consistently generating backward questions that are too easy, too ambiguous, or logically inconsistent with the forward chain — these errors will be baked into the training data and the student will learn to replicate the flawed patterns. This is a form of compounding distillation error: unlike forward reasoning errors where the student can sometimes be corrected by the ground-truth label, backward reasoning errors have no ground-truth supervision in the standard setup (the backward question is a generated artifact). The paper filters for "correct" forward reasoning in its training data, which partially limits this risk, but systematic biases in the teacher’s backward reasoning style (rather than outright errors) could still shape the student’s learned reasoning patterns in undesirable ways.

**6.** Backward thinking is less applicable in domains where the reverse problem is either under-determined (many different starting points could lead to the same result) or where there is no well-defined inverse operation. For example, in open-ended creative reasoning, a "backward question" from a creative answer is ambiguous — many prompts could have generated the same creative output. Similarly, in probabilistic reasoning tasks where the answer is a probability distribution rather than a definitive conclusion, inverting the reasoning is structurally ill-posed. Backward thinking could also be harmful in adversarial settings where it might teach the model to reason about how to construct problems that yield desired answers, which could backfire in security-sensitive or alignment-critical applications.

**7.** RevThink’s strong out-of-distribution generalization — across held-out datasets from different reasoning domains — suggests that backward reasoning teaches a domain-general reasoning capability rather than domain-specific skills. If it were domain-specific, performance gains would be localized to the training distribution and would not transfer to unseen problem structures. The more plausible interpretation is that by learning to verify consistency between forward and backward reasoning chains, the model internalizes a general principle: sound reasoning must be bidirectionally coherent. This is analogous to how mathematical proof training on specific domains can generalize to new domains because it instills reasoning hygiene (logical validity checking) rather than just memorized solution patterns. The 13.53% average improvement across 12 diverse datasets, combined with out-of-distribution gains, supports the view that RevThink builds a transferable metacognitive reasoning skill.
